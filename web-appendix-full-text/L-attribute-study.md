# The Attribute Study: Its Design, Its Results and How the Models Differed

In this file, we describe the attribute study: first its design, then its information conditions and its name
conditions, and last its results. Of the 41 models of Study 1a, 36 enter the estimates of the attribute study: Kimi Linear 48B A3B Instruct is left out as in Study 1a; GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6.2 Sol enter only on the choice sets that each answered in every condition (17 and 25 of the 30); and Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.2 Astra have no responses to it. The models responded to the 30 choice sets of Study 1a in 17
information conditions, each without brand names and with the fictitious name on the optimal product, and in four
further name conditions. The following aspects were identical in all conditions. The optimal product was priced the same
as the cheapest other product, every product had 500 reviews and 4.3 stars unless we changed the reviews in a condition, and
each model responded to each set ten times in each condition, for 11,400
responses a model.

In these information conditions, we gave each of the five pieces alone, as in Study 2a's test of each piece
alone, but to rule out the possibility that the effect of a piece depended on its place, we created two versions of each
piece. In one version, the piece was a row in every product's listing: the three rows of that test, and for the two
pieces given there as sentences, "Figures: from independent lab testing, done the same way for every product" and
"Returns: free, at no cost". In the other version, the piece was a sentence in the shopper's request: the two sentences
of that test, and for the three pieces given there as rows, "Every product below comes with a 2-year warranty.", "An
independent survey found a first-year failure or return rate of 2% of units for every product below." and "Every
product below has been on the market for 10 years or more." With this design, we compare a row with a sentence for each
piece (the table below). We also varied the number of reviews of the optimal product (50 or 5,000, against
500 for every other product). Finally, we added to every product's listing one of four attributes absent from
Study 2a, a certification, an expert review, a country of origin or carbon-neutral manufacturing, which are quoted word
for word in Web Appendix L. In the 17th condition the listings had no information, as in
Study 1a.

We completed a 2 (the optimal product's name: fictitious vs. familiar) by 2 (the other products' names: familiar vs.
fictitious) design with three of the further name conditions, all with no information, and the condition with the
fictitious name on the optimal product and familiar names on the other products, which we asked again in this study. The familiar
name on the optimal product was a familiar brand of the category that was not otherwise in the set (Lenovo in
Laptops 1), and the other products' names in the fictitious condition were fictitious names (Presswell, Thermalux,
Dentara and Sonique in Laptops 1). In the fourth further name condition, we swapped the optimal product's name with a
familiar product's name, as in Study 1a.

We computed the effect of the fictitious name as in Study 2a, at one price. With no information, the fictitious name
lowered the optimal product's choice share by 5.6 percentage points (95% CI =
[3.1, 8.5], *p* < .001).
No piece of information in either version, number of reviews or added attribute changed this effect after Holm correction across the 16 conditions; the smallest Holm-corrected *p* was that of the statement in the request that every figure came from independent laboratory tests (difference = −2.5 percentage points, 95% CI = [−4.7, −0.4], Holm-corrected *p* = .304). For no piece did a row and a sentence differ after Holm
correction (the table below). In the 2 by 2 design, familiar names on the other products lowered the
optimal product's share by about as much as the fictitious name did (difference = −5.9 percentage points, 95% CI = [−8.8, −3.3], Holm-corrected *p* < .001), whereas a familiar name on the optimal product did not raise its share (difference =
−0.3 percentage points, 95% CI =
[−2.7, 1.9], Holm-corrected *p*
= 1.000). Likewise, when the optimal product's name was
swapped with a familiar product's, its share was 67.2 percent, against
66.9 percent with the fictitious name. In this study, then, the
optimal product lost choices to competitors with familiar names, whatever its own name was. In three sets the
fictitious name is also a real company's (Novatech in Laptops 2, Primebook in Laptops 3 and Sonance in Earbuds 2).
Without those sets, the effect was 6.3 points, the two estimates
of the 2 by 2 design were −6.5 and
−0.3 points,
and no condition changed the effect after Holm correction (the smallest Holm-corrected *p*, that of 5,000 reviews of the optimal product, was .144). In the second table below, we report
both estimates of the 2 by 2 design for each model, over all 30 sets.
