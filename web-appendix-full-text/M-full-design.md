# The Choice Sets, Prices and Items of the Full Design in Study 2b

## Choice Sets of the Full Design

For each of seven categories, we showed a model choice sets of five products, each with a brand name, three attributes
of three levels, a star rating of 4.2, 4.4 or 4.6 from 500 reviews and a price, and the model recommended one of them.
The categories were laptops, smartphones, televisions, over-ear headphones, wireless earbuds, running shoes and coffee
makers. Each category had four to six familiar brands, 38 pairs of a brand and a category in all, and two
or three fictitious names, 17 in all. In a web search and in four public collections of web
text, we found no company or product of any fictitious name in its category. We report each category's names and prices in
the table below, its attributes and their levels in the table below and the shopper's
requests in the table below.

The prices cover each category's market. For each category, we recorded the price of the cheapest configuration of
every product at one large US retailer, Walmart for six categories and Zappos for running shoes, and we set the market
range from the 5th to the 95th percentile of those prices. A familiar brand's price band is the middle half of its own
prices in the category, from the 25th to the 75th percentile, and the band's center is their median. For a brand
with fewer than five products at the retailer, we took its band from its own US online store
(9 bands), and for Insignia, which is Best Buy's own brand, from Best Buy. Where a band lay outside the market
range, we widened the range to hold it. Each category had six prices, spaced evenly in log price over the range and
each rounded to the whole dollar less one cent. Three bands lie between two of these prices and contain none of them
(Samsung in earbuds, and Bose and Beyerdynamic in headphones), so the equities of these brands at their band's center
are estimated between two prices in the choice sets.

We built each choice set in two steps. First, we drew each product's level on each attribute, its rating and its
price at random. We drew the set again when one product was at least as good as another on all three attributes, the
rating and the price and better on one of them, or when two products were the same on all five. We also drew it again
when a product had one of the two highest prices and the lowest level of every attribute, or one of the two lowest
prices and the highest level of every attribute. Second, we assigned five of the category's names to the products.
These were the five names used least often so far, with ties broken at random, and of twenty random assignments of
them to the products, the display positions and the letters, we kept the one that left each name's counts at each
price, attribute level, rating, position and letter the most even. A name was therefore close to independent of the
product that it was given: over a category's choice sets, no count of a name at a level differed from the count
expected under independence by more than 1.7% in the main version and
8.6% in any version.

The main version had 1,600 choice sets in each category, each also without brand
names, with the shopper's request in its first wording and without a budget, in a table with one row for each product,
and under one of four wordings of the instruction to the model. 200 of each
category's choice sets were shown a second time, to measure how often a model recommends the same product again. Each
of six further versions, of 800 choice sets in each category, differed from the
main version in one feature. The first had no prices. In the second, the request included the pretest's budget and we
showed four prices inside it, so the budget and the range of prices change together. In the third, the request had its second
wording; in the fourth, each product was a block of text instead of a row; in the fifth, a choice set had three
products instead of five; and in the sixth, the request included a budget that spans the market. A model also rated single products from 1 to 7, and it
answered the choice sets of the direct price premium described below.

## Collection of the Prices

We describe how we collected the retailer prices from which we set each category's market range and each brand's band.
All prices were collected on 22 November 2026 from the sites of Walmart (laptops, smartphones, televisions, over-ear
headphones, wireless earbuds and coffee makers), Zappos (running shoes), Best Buy (Insignia's televisions) and the US
online stores of Apple, Audio-Technica, Beyerdynamic, Bose, Breville, Google, Jabra and Sennheiser. A script requested
each search page or category page with the identification string of a desktop browser, waited four seconds between
requests, stopped at the first page that asked whether the visitor was a person, and saved every page with the time
at which it was retrieved. A second script then extracted the products listed on each saved page from the page's product
data: the name, the price, the seller, the condition, how the order is fulfilled and whether the product was in stock. The brands' stores and Best Buy were loaded in a visible browser controlled by a script (Playwright), or
by a plain request where the page allowed it.

We counted a listing as a product when it was new, sold or fulfilled by the retailer, in stock, priced outright rather
than in monthly installments, and of the category's type of product, not an accessory, a set of products sold together
or a model for children. The listings of one product in several configurations (memory, storage, color or carrier)
count once, at the cheapest configuration. In all, we counted 2,281 products and left out
1,059 listings, each with its reason. A category's market range rests on
160 to 640 products. A brand's band rests on
a median of 14 products, from 1 (Jabra in
earbuds and Beyerdynamic in headphones, whose stores each list one product) to
78, and 6 bands rest on
fewer than five products.

The saved pages are not shared, because their contents belong to the retailers. The price table, with the name, price,
address and time of every product counted and of every listing left out, and the scripts that retrieved and parsed the
pages are shared with the paper's data.
