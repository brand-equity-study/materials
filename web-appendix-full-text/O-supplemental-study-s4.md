# The Full Method and Results of Supplemental Study S4

## Supplemental Study S4

### Method

In S4, we asked whether people in the models' role choose the top-ranked product as often as the models did on
the same tables. Of 1,200 participants recruited on Prolific, 1,182 remained after the
preregistered exclusions. Each saw one of five tables of Study 1b's corpus, with four familiar brands and the
top-ranked product under a fictitious name, the same rating and number of reviews for every product, and a glossary of
the attributes on a page before the choice. The five tables were those on which the models least often chose the
top-ranked product when the shopper's priorities were ranked with the brand last; we selected them using only the models'
responses.
Participants either advised a friend who had written the shopper's request or chose for themselves, under one of three
statements of the shopper's priorities: the needs alone, the needs with the statement that the brand was irrelevant,
or numeric weights for each attribute with the brand at zero. The models' rate on the same tables is that of the 30
models of Study 1b that we ran before the study, with an interval from a bootstrap over the models.
The models received the first statement as people did. At the second level, the models received a ranked list
of the shopper's priorities with the brand last, where people saw a sentence that the brand was irrelevant. At the
third level, the models received a threshold for each attribute and were told that the user had
verified that one product met all of them.

### Results and Discussion

At the first two levels, participants chose the top-ranked product in
69.1% of cases and the models in 39.6% on average over models
(difference before rounding = 29.6 points, 95% CI = [23.5, 35.6], one-sided *p* = 5.0 × 10^−22^), the
preregistered test, and the difference exceeded the preregistered margin of 10 points (*p*
= 1.0 × 10^−10^). Participants who advised a friend chose it in
75.4% of cases, and those who chose for themselves in 62.6%
(the models: 39.6% and 39.5% on
average over models). In the preregistered test within each role, we found the difference in both, below the
preregistered alpha of .005 (advising a friend: difference = 35.8 points, 95% CI =
[29.2, 42.4], one-sided *p* = 3.0 × 10^−26^; choosing for
oneself: difference = 23.1 points, 95% CI = [16.0, 30.1],
one-sided *p* = 7.0 × 10^−11^). With numeric weights, participants chose it in
66.1% of cases; the models, which had been told which product met the user's
requirements, chose it in 99.7%. In the preregistered test of disclosure, we
compared, among those who chose another product, how often people and models named the brand as a reason and how
often they claimed that their choice was better on a feature on which, in the table, it was not, in explanations
coded by five raters who did not know their source, with a majority rule. Participants named the brand more often
than the models (35.8% vs. 10.7%; odds ratio =
4.65, one-sided *p* = 9.7 × 10^−16^), below the preregistered alpha of
.005. Their rate of false feature claims was not reliably lower than the models' at that alpha
(12.5% vs.
17.4%; odds ratio = 0.68, one-sided *p*
= .053).
Of the participants, 88.7% answered the comprehension question correctly, and among
them the difference at the first two levels was 32.6 points.
